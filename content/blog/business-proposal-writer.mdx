"Business proposal writer" is searched by someone with a scope in their head, a deadline, and a blank document. The blank document is the easy problem: proposal structure is one of the most settled conventions in business writing — [APMP](https://www.apmp.org/), the professional body for bid work, exists partly because the shape is stable enough to train people in.

The hard part is that everyone bidding against you knows the same shape, so filling it from a template makes your proposal the third interchangeable PDF that week. This post is about what structure can't fix: which two sections actually carry your voice, what that voice has to sound like, and the same engagement written both ways so the gap is visible rather than asserted.

## What sections does a business proposal need?

**Six or seven, in a fixed order: executive summary, the client's problem, your proposed solution, timeline, pricing, expected outcomes, and — if you're responding to an RFP — a compliance section.** Almost all of that is mechanical. Only two parts actually differentiate you.

| Section | What it does | Should you template it? |
|---|---|---|
| Executive summary | The whole proposal in one page, readable alone | **No.** Often the only page a decision-maker reads |
| The problem | Their situation in their words, before your pitch | **No.** This is where you prove you listened |
| Proposed solution | Scope, approach, phases, what's excluded | Structure yes, framing no |
| Timeline | Dates, milestones, dependencies on them | Yes, fully |
| Pricing | Fees, payment terms, what triggers a change order | Yes, fully — and never bury it |
| Expected outcomes | What's measurably different in 6–12 months | Structure yes |
| Team / credentials / compliance | Bios, references, RFP-required checkboxes | Yes — write once, reuse forever |

Length follows the same logic: 5–15 pages depending on complexity, and shorter almost always wins because decision-makers skim. One exception overrides everything else — if you're answering an RFP, its stated section order and page limits are the rules. Federal solicitations make this explicit: [FAR Part 15](https://www.acquisition.gov/far/part-15) has the government tell you how proposals will be evaluated, and a technically superb bid that ignores the required structure gets scored against the structure it ignored.

## Why do AI-written proposals lose to worse-written ones?

**AI-written proposals lose because they are interchangeable — a proposal is never read alone, and template language collapses three bids for the same work into one document in the reader's memory.** Fluency isn't the differentiator; specificity is.

Government buying formalises exactly that comparison. Under [FAR Part 15](https://www.acquisition.gov/far/part-15), an agency evaluating competitive proposals assesses their *relative* qualities solely against the factors and subfactors stated in the solicitation (FAR 15.305) — a scoring exercise in which prose polish is not a factor and an unsupported superlative earns nothing. The same discipline applies outside government bids: an [investor update](/write/investor-update) or a [cold email](/write/cold-email) fails the same way a proposal does, for the same reason. Private buyers run the same comparison less formally and land in the same place: the reader is choosing between documents, not grading one in isolation.

The fingerprints of a bid that loses that comparison are consistent enough to spot from the executive summary:

- **"We are uniquely positioned to…"** — an assertion where a reason should be, and every bidder makes it.
- **"Leverage our best-in-class methodology to drive synergies."** Four abstractions, zero nouns the client can picture.
- **The problem section describing a category, not a company.** "In today's competitive logistics landscape" means you didn't take notes in the discovery call.
- **Outcomes with no units.** "Significant efficiency gains," "improved visibility," "streamlined operations."
- **Pricing on page 11, unlabeled.** Skimmers hunt for it first; hiding it reads as a flinch.
- **Passive scope.** "Deliverables will be provided on a mutually agreed timeline" — nobody is responsible for anything in that sentence.

## What tone should a business proposal use?

**The tone your team already uses explaining the work out loud to a client who trusts you — plain, specific, active, confident without superlatives.** Proposal tone drifts formal because writers mistake formality for credibility, and a general-purpose AI tool amplifies the drift, because formal-generic is the genre's statistical average and the average is what a model without your samples has to reach for.

That drift is well enough established that the US government legislated against it in its own writing: the Plain Writing Act of 2010 obliges federal agencies to write in plain language, and the resulting [federal plain-language guidelines](https://www.plainlanguage.gov/guidelines/) are a fair proxy for the target here — short sentences, active voice, concrete nouns, everyday words, no hedging. A rule that had to be written into law is a rule professional writing breaks by default; a proposal is one of the places it breaks hardest.

The gap between a template's tone and yours is purely an input difference. A template starts from a brief and fills the blanks with genre-average phrasing; a voice profile starts from writing you've already done, so the average never gets a vote.

## What does the same proposal look like in two voices?

**Almost identical on the facts and unrecognisable on the page: the templated version opens by describing the firm, and the voice-matched version opens with something only someone who visited the client's dock could write.** Below is one executive summary twice over, same engagement both times — a 12-week operations review for a regional freight company, fixed fee $48,000. The example is illustrative rather than a real client, but the gap between the two versions is the whole argument.

**Template output:**

> *Acme Consulting is pleased to present this proposal for a comprehensive operational assessment. As a leading provider of supply-chain advisory services, we are uniquely positioned to leverage our proven methodology to unlock significant efficiencies across your organization. Our best-in-class team will partner with your stakeholders to deliver actionable insights that drive sustainable, long-term value.*

**Voice-matched, from how the firm's partners actually write:**

> *You're paying overtime on Saturdays to move freight that was supposed to leave on Thursday. We think the delay starts at the dock, not in dispatch — and you'll know within four weeks whether we're right. Twelve weeks, $48,000 fixed, three deliverables: a measured dock-to-departure baseline, a ranked list of what's causing the slip, and the two fixes we'd make first. If the baseline shows dispatch is the problem instead, we'll tell you that and stop.*

The second version invents nothing the first one had. It names one observable fact, commits to a falsifiable finding, states the price in the first ten lines, and says out loud what it won't do — and it survives being read next to two competitors, because no competitor could have written that opening sentence.

That's the split worth internalising: structure is a solved problem, tone is not. It's the division the [business proposal writer](/write/business-proposal) is built around — the seven-section skeleton is fixed, and the voice comes from samples of your own writing rather than from a brief ([pricing](/pricing) lists what each plan includes). The same division runs through every [business document](/write/business) around a deal, most sharply through the [executive summary](/write/executive-summary) — often the only section a decision-maker reads end to end.

## What prompt produces a usable proposal draft?

**This one — and the work is done by what it forbids and what it feeds in, not by the format instructions.**

```
Draft a business proposal, 1,200-1,500 words. Sections, in this order:
executive summary, the client's problem, proposed solution (with phases
and explicit exclusions), timeline, pricing, expected outcomes.

Use ONLY the facts below. Do not invent metrics, past results, client
names, certifications, team size, or prices. If something is missing,
write [GAP: what's needed] instead of filling it in.

- Client + what they do: [...]
- The problem in their own words, from the discovery call: [...]
- One observable fact about their operation I can name: [...]
- What we will do, in phases: [...]
- What we will NOT do (explicit exclusions): [...]
- Timeline + fee + payment terms: [...]
- What is measurably different in 6-12 months: [...]
- RFP-required section names or page limits, if any: [...]

Banned phrases: "uniquely positioned", "best-in-class", "leading
provider", "leverage", "synergies", "world-class", "seamless",
"holistic", "actionable insights", "drive value", "in today's".
No outcome stated without a unit. No passive-voice deliverables.

Write in this voice. Here is how we actually write — match the rhythm,
sentence length, and vocabulary:
[paste 2-3 paragraphs from a real client email, memo, or past proposal]
```

<Callout>
**The `[GAP]` line matters more here than in any other document type.** A model's instinct is to smooth a missing number into a plausible one — and in a proposal, a plausible invented number is a price or a timeline a client may hold you to. Forcing the gap into the open converts invention into a short checklist only you can close: eight bracketed blanks you fill from your own records, rather than eight confident sentences you have to catch on a reread.
</Callout>

## What should you never let an AI proposal writer do?

**Never let it supply a fact.** Every serious failure mode is a version of that one rule, and a proposal is a higher-stakes place to break it than most documents, because a client can treat it as a commitment.

- **Invented past results.** "We reduced costs 30% for a similar client" is either a reference you can produce or a lie.
- **Invented client names or logos.** Using a name as social proof without permission is its own problem, separate from accuracy.
- **Invented credentials.** Certifications, clearances, insurance limits, headcount, years in business.
- **Prices and dates you haven't staffed.** The draft doesn't know your utilisation; you do.
- **Rewritten legal or compliance language.** Terms, liability caps, and RFP-mandated wording get pasted, not regenerated. In a federal bid this is not a style question: [FAR Part 15](https://www.acquisition.gov/far/part-15) has the solicitation set the required representations and the stated evaluation factors, and a model that paraphrases them has quietly made your bid non-responsive on a point no evaluator will let slide.

Draft freely, then verify every number and every claim about your own firm against something you actually have. That pass takes ten minutes and is the difference between a fast proposal and an expensive one.

## FAQ

**What is a business proposal writer?**
A tool or person that turns your scope, pricing, and timeline into the standard proposal format. The format is public convention, so framing and tone are the only parts that separate your proposal from a competitor's.

**What sections does a business proposal need?**
Executive summary, the problem, your proposed solution, timeline, pricing, and expected outcomes — plus a compliance section if you're answering an RFP. Lead with value for the client, not features about you.

**How long should a business proposal be?**
5–15 pages depending on complexity, and shorter usually wins because decision-makers skim. An RFP's own page and section limits override that.

**What tone should a business proposal use?**
The tone your team uses explaining the work out loud: plain, specific, active, no superlatives. Bid boilerplate reads generic because every bidder generates it from the same template.

**Can AI write a business proposal?**
Yes — the structural sections are the part it does best, since they follow a fixed convention. It can't supply your prices, staffing, or past results, and it won't sound like your firm unless you give it your writing.

**What should never go in an AI-drafted proposal?**
An invented result, client name, certification, headcount, or price. Verify anything the client could later hold you to.
