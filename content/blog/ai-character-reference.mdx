A character reference is one of the few documents where *who wrote it* matters as much as what it says. A judge, hiring manager, or landlord is reading it to answer a single question: does a real, credible person actually vouch for this human? Which is exactly why a reference that reads like it was generated in five seconds fails — it answers that question with "apparently not."

This guide covers what a character reference is, who should write one, what to include for each situation, and how to use AI to beat the blank page without stripping out the thing that makes it work.

## What is a character reference?

A character reference is a short personal statement from someone who knows you outside of work, describing what you're actually like as a person. Unlike a professional reference — which covers job performance — it speaks to honesty, reliability, and conduct, and it can come from a neighbor, a coach, a teacher, or a friend of fifteen years. [Indeed's career guide draws the same line](https://www.indeed.com/career-advice/career-development/character-reference-letter) between the two documents.

An **AI character reference** is that same document drafted with help from an AI tool: the AI handles structure and phrasing, you supply every fact, example, and judgment. The AI is the assistant. You're still the person putting your name on it, and your credibility is the entire asset being transferred.

## Who can write a character reference?

Anyone who knows the person well in a non-professional capacity and can describe things they personally witnessed. The best referees have three traits: a clear relationship to the person, enough time to have seen them under pressure, and something at stake in their own reputation. Where a court is involved, start with that court's own published guidance on who it will accept — [GOV.UK's page on character references for court](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/character-references-for-court) is the model for the UK, and most jurisdictions publish an equivalent.

Absent a rule from the reader, here's how we'd rank the options:

- **Strongest:** a neighbor, coach, teacher, clergy member, volunteer coordinator, or longtime friend who can name dates and specific incidents.
- **Good:** a former colleague speaking to character rather than performance, or a community member who's seen the person's conduct over years.
- **Weakest:** immediate family. We'd steer you away from a parent, sibling, or spouse where you have any choice — a reader has no way to separate love from assessment, so it's the weakest slot to spend. Use family only if the process specifically asks for it.

One rule overrides the rest: don't write one you don't believe. A lukewarm reference reads as lukewarm no matter how it's phrased, and a reader who senses hedging discounts everything above it.

## What should a character reference include?

Every strong character reference answers four questions in order: who you are, how you know the person, what they're actually like (with examples), and why you're vouching for them. That's not a novel framework — writing and career guidance converge on the same shape, from the [Purdue OWL's conventions for recommendation letters](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/common_writing_assignments/index.html) to [Indeed's breakdown of the format](https://www.indeed.com/career-advice/career-development/character-reference-letter).

What changes between situations isn't the structure, it's the emphasis. The table below is our read of what each reader is weighing, not a list of filing requirements — where the recipient publishes its own rules, those win:

| Situation | What the reader is usually weighing | What to lead with |
|---|---|---|
| **Court / legal** | Sober, observed behavior — not praise | How long you've known them, and that you're aware of the charge |
| **Employment** | Reliability and conduct under pressure | A witnessed example of follow-through |
| **Rental / tenancy** | Responsibility, communication, payment | Concrete tenancy behavior ("gave 60 days' notice, left it spotless") |
| **Immigration / visa** | A credible, documented relationship | How you met, how often you're in contact, verifiable detail |
| **School / volunteer** | Maturity and how they treat others | An example involving other people, not achievements |

Court references carry the most rules and the least room for error — address the letter the way that specific court requires, state that you know what the person has been charged with, and never suggest a sentence or minimize the offense. [GOV.UK's guidance on character references for court](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/character-references-for-court) is a solid model even outside the UK, and it's worth reading before you draft anything for a legal proceeding.

The operating principle we'd write every version of this document by: **specificity beats vocabulary.** A reference built on two concrete, firsthand examples is more persuasive than one stacked with generic praise ("hardworking, honest, kind") — because details are what a stranger can't fake, and what AI leaves out unless you supply them. Change the situation and the facts change; the part where details do the persuading never does.

## How long should a character reference be?

If the reader hasn't specified a format, aim for one page — our recommendation is roughly 250 to 400 words. Two observed examples and an honest assessment is enough for any of the contexts above, and going longer almost always means padding with adjectives. Where a court, agency, or letting agent publishes its own length and addressing rules, follow those over anything here.

Length is also a credibility signal. A reader who's seen a hundred of these can usually tell that three pages of praise means the writer had one thin anecdote and stretched it.

## How do you write an AI character reference that doesn't sound AI-generated?

Feed the AI *your* raw material and *your* voice instead of asking it to invent both. Generic prompts produce generic references, and generic is the specific failure mode a character reference can't survive.

Here's the difference in practice. Both passages below are **invented illustrations** written for this guide — "Maria" is not a real person and neither reference was submitted anywhere.

**Default AI output (what to avoid):**

> *I am writing to wholeheartedly recommend Maria as a person of outstanding character. She has consistently demonstrated integrity, dedication, and a strong work ethic. She would be a valuable asset in any capacity.*

That could be about anyone. It contains zero verifiable information — nothing a reader could check, and nothing they couldn't have written themselves.

**The same fictional reference, with details and voice supplied:**

> *I've rented the unit above Maria for three years and I run the building's tenant association. When a pipe burst on her floor last winter, she was the one who caught it at 2 a.m., shut off the main, and texted every neighbor on the line before she'd even called the landlord. She's the tenant who leaves a place better than she found it.*

Same length. One is forgettable; the other would be impossible to fake if it were real. The second version only exists because the writer handed the AI the actual story and let the tool shape it in their natural, plainspoken register — which is the part most tools get wrong, because their default register is nobody's.

## A prompt you can copy and adapt

A good character-reference prompt does two things: it forbids the AI from inventing anything, and it forces you to hand over the specifics only you know. Fill in the brackets — the more real detail you give it, the less generic the draft.

```
Help me write a one-page character reference for [name], 250–400 words.
Use only what I give you. Do not invent any relationship, date, or event.
If something is missing, ask me instead of filling it in.

- How I know them: [neighbor / coach / coworker / friend]
- How long, and how often we're in contact: [3 years, weekly]
- What it's for: [job / court / rental / visa / school / volunteer role]
- 2 specific things I personally witnessed: [story 1], [story 2]
- My honest overall read on their character: [in my own words]
- Anything I must acknowledge up front: [e.g. I'm aware of the charge]

Voice: match the writing sample below. Warm but plain. Short sentences.
No clichés — no "asset in any capacity," no "wholeheartedly recommend,"
no lists of adjectives without an example behind them.

Writing sample:
[paste 3–4 sentences of something you actually wrote]
```

That last block is the one people skip and the one that matters most. Pasting in three or four sentences of your own writing — a long text, an email, a review you left — is the single step that separates a reference sounding like *you* from one sounding like a form letter. Our [companion walkthrough of the drafting process](/blog/ai-character-reference-letter) works through the same prompt line by line if you want more on structure and editing.

## What makes a character reference get ignored?

The most common failure is shipping the first AI draft unedited, because default AI output has exactly the qualities a reader uses to dismiss a reference: praise with no proof, no situational awareness, and an identical tone for every person and every purpose.

- **Adjectives with no evidence.** Delete any trait you can't back with a one-line example.
- **Invented specifics.** If the AI adds a date, title, or event you didn't supply, cut it. A single wrong detail voids the whole document.
- **Wrong register for the context.** A rental reference is not a court reference. Tell the AI the situation before it drafts, not after.
- **Overstatement in legal contexts.** Never minimize an offense or recommend an outcome — sentencing isn't your role, and arguing it reads as advocacy rather than observation. Check the [published guidance for the court in question](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/character-references-for-court) before you write a word.
- **No out-loud pass.** Read it aloud. If a sentence isn't something you'd actually say, rewrite it in words you would.

## FAQ

**What is an AI character reference?**
It's a personal reference drafted with help from an AI tool, where the AI handles structure and phrasing but every fact, example, and opinion comes from you. You stay the author of record.

**Is it okay to use AI to write a character reference?**
Yes, as long as everything in it is true and it reflects your genuine opinion. AI is a drafting tool; the ethical line is honest versus fabricated, not AI versus no AI.

**Who can write a character reference?**
Anyone who knows the person well outside of work and can describe things they personally witnessed — a neighbor, coach, teacher, clergy member, or longtime friend. We'd avoid immediate family where you have a choice, since a reader can't separate love from assessment.

**How long should a character reference be?**
Our recommendation is one page — roughly 250 to 400 words. Two concrete examples and an honest assessment are enough for any context. Follow the reader's own format rules where they publish them.

**Can I use AI for a character reference for court?**
You can use it to draft, but be conservative: firsthand examples only, correct addressing, a sober tone, and no minimizing the offense or suggesting a sentence.

**What's the difference between a character reference and a professional reference?**
A professional reference speaks to your work performance and skills. A character reference speaks to who you are as a person — honesty, reliability, conduct — and can come from anyone who knows you well, not just an employer.
